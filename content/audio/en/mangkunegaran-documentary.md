## Opening {#opening}

> SOUND: Quiet ambient room tone. A single, soft sustained tone on a bronze instrument, fading under the first sentence.

Surakarta — also called Solo — lies in the heart of Central Java. For more than two and a half centuries, two royal houses have shared this city. One is the Kasunanan, the court of the Sunan. The other, younger house is Mangkunegaran.

This is the story of Mangkunegaran: how it was born out of war, how it lived inside a colonial order, how it turned culture into a modern project, and how, after Indonesian independence, it became something different again — a house that keeps a heritage rather than governs a territory.

We will follow the evidence: the institution's own published history, historians' studies, legal records and archival photographs. Where those sources disagree, we will say so.

> PAUSE: 2 seconds.

## Chapter 1 — Origins {#foundation}

> SOUND: The ambient tone thins out. Silence for a moment before the chapter begins.

In the eighteenth century, the great Javanese kingdom of Mataram was no longer secure. According to Mangkunegaran's own account, its political and social stability was weakening as the Dutch East India Company — the VOC — intervened in the affairs of the court.

Into this world, on the seventh of April seventeen twenty-five, a boy was born in Kartasura, the court capital. His name was Raden Mas Said. His father was Kanjeng Pangeran Arya Mangkoenagoro, remembered as Mangkoenagoro Sepuh; his mother was Raden Ayu Wulan. The official history records that his mother died after his birth, and that the VOC exiled his father to Sri Lanka. These details come from a single source, the official site, and we present them as that source gives them.

As a young man, Raden Mas Said took up arms against the VOC. His resistance began, the official history says, with a core of forty loyal followers, and it was fought as a guerrilla war. Its motto was *Tiji Tibeh* — *mukti siji mukti kabeh, mati siji mati kabeh*: one prospers, all prosper; one falls, all fall. He is remembered by another name, too: Pangeran Sambernyawa.

> PAUSE: 1 second.

In seventeen fifty-five, at a village called Giyanti, the Company, the Sunan Pakubuwana the Third, and Pangeran Mangkubumi agreed to divide Mataram. Pakubuwana kept Surakarta. Mangkubumi became Sultan Hamengkubuwana the First of Yogyakarta. Raden Mas Said was not a party to that agreement. He fought on.

Two years later, in March seventeen fifty-seven, a settlement was reached at Salatiga. Raden Mas Said came to terms with Pakubuwana the Third, with Hamengkubuwana the First and the VOC represented. Sources differ on the exact day — the official site gives the seventeenth of March; an older city-government account gives the thirteenth.

What the sources agree on is the outcome. Raden Mas Said became Kanjeng Gusti Pangeran Adipati Arya Mangkunegara the First. He held the rank of *Pangeran Miji*, directly after the crown prince, with a claim to four thousand *cacah* — households — in territories that included Kaduang, Nglaroh, Matesih, Gunung Kidul, Kedu and parts of Pajang. Mangkunegaran was an autonomous *kadipaten*, with the right to keep its own army.

He reigned until seventeen ninety-five. Almost two centuries later, in nineteen eighty-eight, the Republic of Indonesia named him a National Hero.

> PAUSE: 2 seconds before the chapter transition.

## Chapter 2 — The Pura {#pura}

> SOUND: Light, open room tone; a faint impression of outdoor space. No birdsong effects that suggest a specific place.

Construction of the palace — the *Pura* — began in seventeen fifty-seven, under the first Mangkunegara. Like the royal kraton, it was laid out as a sequence of spaces within high walls.

First comes the *pamedan*: an open field inside the gate, where, for generations, the soldiers of Mangkunegaran trained. Beyond it stands the *Pendhapa Ageng*, the great hall: a joglo-roofed pavilion of about three thousand five hundred square metres, built without nails, able to hold some five to ten thousand people. Its wooden pillars came from a forest in the Wonogiri hills; the sources name different forests, so we name only the hills.

Behind the hall is the *pringgitan*, an open veranda where *wayang kulit* — shadow-puppet theatre — is performed. And beyond that, up a flight of steps, is the *Dalem Ageng*, the main house, traditionally the royal bridal chamber and today a museum of the house's heirlooms and portraits.

The buildings visitors see today are not a single moment in time. Photographs held at Leiden University, taken by the studio Woodbury and Page before eighteen seventy-four, show the pendhapa with a long, low roof and a great tree beside it. Later photographs show the same hall with lamps, statues and a changing forecourt. The Pura has been rebuilt, extended and reinterpreted by successive rulers.

> PAUSE: 1 second.

## Chapter 3 — Court, colony and enterprise {#court}

> SOUND: A slow, sparse instrumental texture may enter here, very low. Keep it abstract.

Through the nineteenth century, Mangkunegaran operated inside the colonial order of the Dutch East Indies. Its army was formally named the *Legiun Mangkunegaran* by a decree of Governor-General Daendels, dated the twenty-ninth of July eighteen-oh-eight, during the reign of Mangkunegara the Second. European officers were invited to train it. The Legion protected the domain — and, as the official history puts it, served as a source of legitimacy for the ruler, within a colonial framework that sanctioned it.

Under Mangkunegara the Fourth, who reigned from eighteen fifty-three to eighteen eighty-one, the house turned to enterprise. On the eighth of December eighteen sixty-one, ground was broken for a sugar mill at Colomadu; it began production the following year. The historian Wasino describes this sugar industry as a response to the spread of Western private plantations — beginning as the ruler's personal enterprise, later becoming an enterprise of the state, the *praja*.

The same ruler founded the palace library, Rekso Pustoko, in eighteen sixty-seven. It still keeps leather-bound manuscripts, books in Javanese, photographs and plantation records. And he is remembered as a writer: *Serat Wedhatama*, one hundred stanzas of moral guidance, is attributed to him.

> PAUSE: 1 second.

Mangkunegaran's arts also began to travel. A photograph from eighteen seventy-nine shows a large gamelan from the Mangkunegaran court at the National Exhibition in Arnhem, in the Netherlands. Another, from the Paris world's fair of eighteen eighty-nine, shows four dancers from the court. The catalogue gives their names: Taminah, Sariem, Soekia and Wakiem.

## Chapter 4 — Culture as a modern project {#modern}

In nineteen sixteen, Mangkunegara the Seventh became the reigning prince, and the Legion paraded at the palace to mark the occasion. His reign, until nineteen forty-four, treated Javanese culture as something to study and to renew. After a congress on Javanese culture in nineteen eighteen, he recommended a research institute; the Java Instituut was founded the following year. In nineteen thirty-three, with his support, a radio society was founded in Surakarta — the Solosche Radio Vereeniging — described as the first radio station in the Dutch East Indies run by indigenous people. And in nineteen thirty-nine, a court dance he created, *Bedhaya Bedhah Madiun*, was performed for the first time. It is still performed today.

> PAUSE: 2 seconds. This is the major transition of the documentary.

## Chapter 5 — Within the Republic {#republic}

> SOUND: The instrumental texture stops. A moment of near-silence.

In August nineteen forty-five, Indonesia declared its independence. On the eighteenth of August, Mangkunegara the Eighth and Pakubuwana the Twelfth of the Kasunanan sent their congratulations. On the first of September, Mangkunegaran declared its support for the Republic.

Independence did not preserve the old order. Amid a social revolution in Surakarta, a government regulation dated the fifteenth of July nineteen forty-six made Surakarta a residency directly under the Republic. The Kasunanan and Mangkunegaran lost their political authority. The official site dates the social revolution in two different ways on two different pages — nineteen forty-five to forty-six, and nineteen forty-five to nineteen fifty. The legal turning point is clear; the wider period is described differently.

In nineteen fifty, a new law placed the Surakarta area in the province of Central Java. Unlike Yogyakarta, Surakarta did not become a special region.

> PAUSE: 1 second.

What continued was the house itself. Mangkunegara the Eighth died in nineteen eighty-seven. His son succeeded him and was formally installed on the twenty-fourth of January nineteen eighty-eight — the first head of the house installed after independence. As Mangkunegara the Ninth, he led arts missions to France, England and Japan, created new dance works, and opened the palace to artists from outside. Dance is still performed every thirty-five days to mark his birth. He died in August twenty twenty-one, and his son became Mangkunegara the Tenth.

In January twenty twenty-three, a new garden opened on the western side of the palace, Pracima Tuin. And in April twenty twenty-four, at a dinner marking the house's two hundred and sixty-seventh anniversary, a welcome song first composed for King Chulalongkorn of Siam was performed again — its nineteenth-century text transliterated, translated and adapted for today.

## Closing {#closing}

> SOUND: Return to the opening room tone. A single soft tone, as at the start, under the last paragraph.

Mangkunegaran began as a settlement at the end of a war. It became a court within a colony, a patron of new culture, and finally a house without political power that still holds a great deal of meaning.

Its history is not one smooth line. It includes conflict, colonial rule, revolution, and loss as well as continuity. Keeping heritage honestly means keeping all of that — the photographs and the gaps, the sources that agree and the ones that do not.

In a country whose motto is *Bhinneka Tunggal Ika* — unity in diversity — houses like Mangkunegaran show that a regional identity can live inside a national one. The palace, its arts and its records belong to Surakarta's story, and to everyone who chooses to listen to it.

> PAUSE: 3 seconds. Fade the room tone out.
